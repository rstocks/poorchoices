A Poor Choice, recounted

I named my band ‘The Variables’

It seemed clever for about eleven seconds, which was, in retrospect, the full lifespan of the band.

By Marlowe Quigg · 18 April 2026 · 3 min “read,” technically

  • music
  • hubris
  • public-shame
The Regret, Catalogued
Regret rating
●●●●●●●●○○ 8/10
Would do again?
No. (Asterisk.)
Lesson learned
If you have to explain the band name to your own bassist, the gig is already going badly.
Cost
One ironed shirt, $40 in flyer printing, my dignity.

The first sign that The Variables was going to be a problem came when our drummer, Pat, asked — reasonably — what the name meant. I said it was a maths joke. Pat said, “Right, but what’s the joke.”

I did not have an answer. I still do not have an answer.

The flyer

I designed the flyer myself, which is its own line item in the broader regret. I used a font I had downloaded that morning. The venue’s owner, a man named Greg who has seen many bands, looked at it for some time and then said: “Mate.”

I assumed this was praise. It was not praise.

We played to thirteen people, four of whom were the other band. One of the remaining nine left during our second song to take a phone call and did not return. I have thought about that phone call almost every day since.

What I would have named us instead

Anything. Genuinely anything. “Two Beers Please.” “The Sound Check.” “Greg.” In hindsight, “Greg” would have at least gotten us a second booking on the strength of the in-joke alone.

The band broke up six weeks later, citing “creative differences,” which is what you say when the creative difference is that one person named the band The Variables.

    About the publication

    An incomplete defence of Poor Choices.

    A periodical, in the loosest possible sense of the word, devoted to the public cataloguing of private misjudgements.

    Poor Choices began, as many poor choices do, after midnight, in a group chat, between two people who should have been asleep. The premise was simple and remains simple: there are decisions, and there are decisions, and most of us are carrying around at least one of the latter that we have never given the dignity of a proper write-up.

    Our contributors are not experts. That is, in fact, the entire qualification. They are people who once said yes when no would have done, or who chose the harp over a savings account, or who hit Reply All on a Tuesday. They have agreed to write about it. They have not, for the most part, agreed to learn from it.

    Editorial principles, such as they are

    • No advice columns. If we knew, we wouldn’t be here.
    • No takedowns. You may only volunteer your own regrets, not others’.
    • No redemption arcs. Sometimes the kayak is just in the living room.
    • No AI confessions. If a machine made the choice, the machine can write about it.

    The masthead

    Poor Choices is edited by a rotating committee of people who answered an email they should not have answered. The masthead changes whenever someone reads their own contribution back and asks to be removed from it. We honour these requests, eventually.

    Submissions

    Submit a regret.

    We welcome contributions from people who have made at least one decision they would, with the benefit of hindsight, like to formally walk back.

    What we’re looking for

    • First-person. Your regret, not your housemate’s.
    • 500–1,500 words. Enough to commit. Not enough to spiral.
    • A regret rating, 1–10. Be honest. We can tell when you’re not.
    • An answer to: “Would you do it again?” “Yes, and probably will” is an acceptable answer.
    • One lesson learned, optional. “None, frankly” is also an acceptable answer.

    What we are not looking for

    • Crimes. (Speak to a lawyer, not us.)
    • Other people’s regrets, dressed up as your own.
    • Things that are obviously a flex pretending to be a regret. We can see you.

    Form is wired for Netlify Forms. By submitting, you agree to be edited, gently, by someone with their own regrets.

    Submission received

    Thank you. We have your regret.

    It will be read, in due course, by a person who has read many such regrets and is, by all accounts, holding up. We’ll be in touch only if we need to clarify a detail or, much more rarely, if we want to publish it.

    In the meantime, you may wish to:

    • Go for a short walk.
    • Re-read what you submitted, just the once, and then close the tab.
    • Resist the urge to email us a follow-up explaining the bit you forgot. (We have all done this. None of us recommend it.)

    What happens next

    Submissions are reviewed by the rotating editorial committee. We aim to respond within a fortnight, but our schedule is, on principle, also one of our regrets.
